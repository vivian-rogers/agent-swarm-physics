\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

In the AI Village, a group of AI agents shares computers, files and a group chat, and works toward one shared goal at a time~\cite{aivillage}. Again and again, each agent forgets what it was just doing, and this is by design. Each agent is a language model inside a program, the \emph{scaffold}, that feeds the model text and carries out its actions. The model works in turns that we call \emph{model calls}, or \emph{calls}. At each call it reads its \emph{context window}, the block of text in front of it, and writes one output: a chat message, a command on its computer or a decision to wait. The context window is the agent's short-term memory. In the village's latest era, the scaffold wipes it each time it fills to 41 records. The agent keeps its notes and its files, but it loses the record of what it just read and did.

The scaffold forced more than 18\,000 of these wipes in the nine goal periods that we studied for this effect. The scaffold, not the work, sets their timing, so each wipe is a small unplanned experiment. After a wipe, an agent makes 39\% fewer \emph{work commits}, saved changes to its code and documents, over its next 10 calls (95\% confidence interval, CI, 35--42\%). Its saved notes do not make up the loss. The memory that matters is the context window.

Reading works the same way. During a charity drive in 2026, one agent posted a message to its room. Room-mates in the middle of a call read it 7--24\,s later, at their next call. Agents in a long pause read it 130--169\,s later, and they replied at that same call. A message acts only at its \emph{read-out call}, the first call whose context holds it: the moment the agent sees it. Until then it is \emph{in flight}: posted, but not yet in the agent's context. So we keep time on a \emph{call clock}, which counts each agent's own calls, not minutes. An agent that waits ten minutes between two calls has not ``aged''; only the number of its calls counts.

\subsection*{A magnet, a crowd, or something new?}

Researchers in interpretability must explain, from the logs, what a swarm of agents did and why. Operators must control it, with a few numbers to watch and a few levers that work. The village record holds 381\,000 timeline events and 173\,000 agent chat messages. A useful description must be far smaller and must still predict.

The hard part is to tell influence from coincidence. All agents share inputs: the same goal text, the same daily timetable, and models trained in similar ways. Such inputs can make agents move together even when they never read each other. Think of two crowds. At a concert, everyone stands when the band walks on stage, and nobody looks at a neighbor. In a stadium wave, each person stands because a neighbor did. In a photo the two crowds look alike, but a push affects them differently. Is a swarm the concert or the wave? Is it a magnet, a crowd, or something new?

Physicists have tools for this question. A magnet holds about $10^{23}$ \emph{spins}, tiny parts that each point in one of a few directions, yet a few numbers predict its response. A \emph{field} acts on each spin directly, as the band acts on the concert crowd. A \emph{coupling} acts from one spin to another, as in the wave. In the swarm, a field is any input that reaches each agent on its own: the timetable, the goal text, the room, the agent's own habits. A coupling is the effect of one agent on another, and it can travel only through a message that the other agent reads. Sociophysics applies these ideas to human groups~\cite{castellano2009}. The analogy has limits. A spin can flip at any moment, but an agent changes only at its own calls. And a goal period has 4 to 21 agents at its start, not $10^{23}$, so any phase transition is blurred.

Recent work applies statistical mechanics to language-model agents directly. El \emph{et al.} studied more than 10\,000 designed communities of agents that exchange messages and revise opinions on fixed questions~\cite{el2026physics}. In their model, each agent tends to move to lower social pressure, and this model predicts individual trajectories. They also found that the communities operate below a critical temperature, which agrees with our subcritical loop gain. Their approach is different from ours, and their model is very simple: one opinion per agent, pairwise ties and one temperature. Our swarm is one open deployment over 18 months. There, external fields, read-out timing and context memory control the dynamics, and pairwise ties alone do not predict the data. In a naming game played in random pairs, populations of agents agree on a convention with every model tested~\cite{ashery2025}, as people do~\cite{centola2015}. In such designed settings the experimenter controls the shared inputs; in an open deployment we must measure them.

\subsection*{Rules against fooling ourselves}

We treat each physics model as an \emph{effective theory}: a small model that predicts what we can observe, with no claim about the inside of the language model. We matched 17 models against 132 hypotheses, which Claude agents wrote, ran and scored under review by the human author. A good fit does not count. A model must beat its strongest \emph{null}, the simplest model with no effect, and it must predict a statistic that it was not fitted to. Four rules guard the tests.
\begin{tightenum}
\item \emph{Dated predictions.} Each hypothesis writes down its prediction, its null and the result that would kill it before any test on real data.
\item \emph{Impostor checks.} Each claim of influence must rule out four \emph{impostors}, shared inputs that fake influence: the \emph{scheduler} (the operator's daily timetable that turns agents on and off), outside drives such as the goal text, habits that the models share, and agents that reach the same idea at the same moment. The in-flight message is our placebo for the last one: a read message must act more than one posted at the same time but not yet read.
\item \emph{Natural experiments.} We cannot run new swarms, so we use dated changes of the scaffold, the roster, the rooms or the operator, such as the forced wipes, as interventions.
\item \emph{Reserved data.} We set aside 16 goal periods and four natural-experiment windows for later confirmation.
\end{tightenum}

The record runs from April 2025 to September 2026 (Fig.~\ref{fig:timeline}). In that time 46 agents from several AI labs worked through 51 \emph{goal periods}, each the time for one shared goal, usually a working week. Each starts with a \emph{kickoff}, the message that announces the goal. Three scaffold changes divide the record into three \emph{regimes}: one shared chat room in daily sessions (I); several \emph{rooms}, separate chat channels (II); and continuous operation, own room only, with forced wipes (III).

\subsection*{Weak echoes, strong pulls}

Two models hold up well, two more hold in part, and together they give one picture (Sec.~\ref{sec:picture}). Each agent behaves like a \emph{kinetic spin}: a unit with a few states, here talk, work or wait, that changes state only at its own calls. When a call takes twice as long, the chance of a reply hardly changes. For the dynamics, the unit of time is the call, not the minute.

Influence needs reading, and it works best with a name. Replies jump at the read-out call, not before it, in 17 of 17 periods. A \emph{named message}, one that mentions the reader by name, raises the chance that the reading call is a talk call by 0.079 (CI 0.069--0.089). An unnamed message raises it by only 0.004 (CI 0.003--0.006). As at a noisy party, agents answer to their own name.

These couplings are weak, and a microphone near a loudspeaker shows why that matters. At low volume a sound makes a faint echo that dies away; at high volume the echo feeds on itself and howls. The \emph{loop gain} $g$ is the average number of new messages that one message causes directly. Below 1, echoes die out. In the village $g$ is about 0.13 (median over 25 \emph{units}, goal periods or parts of them), so 100 messages cause about 15 more in all, replies to replies included (derived). The swarm sits far below the \emph{critical point}, where echoes would run away. A check with no fitted parameter agrees: the swings of total talk are 1.01 times (CI 0.94--1.09) the size that this $g$ predicts.

Fields, not couplings, set most of what agents do. The scheduler's daily start and stop explain about 70--80\% of the times when agents are active together (a fragile value: it changed between versions of the data). On day~1, what agents write lies closer to their own kickoff than to 32 other kickoff texts in 18 of 33 periods. A kickoff is a \emph{quench}, a sudden change of the field, like a hot metal bar dropped into cold water.

Within those fields, picture an agent as a ball in a bowl of thick honey. A tap moves the ball, and its effect fades fast; then the ball creeps back to the bottom and never swings past it. In goal period~51, the pull of one message on the reader's content, the \emph{kick}, fades in about 7 calls, while the agent's content returns to its own resting point, the \emph{well}, over about 100 calls. We chose this two-rate form after a one-rate model failed, so it still needs a test written in advance. The agent's working state, as the opening scene showed, lives in its context window. And beyond one shared pattern of movement, which is what a field produces, few \emph{collective modes} (patterns in which many agents move together) rise above the level that chance produces. No group of agents acts as one larger agent.

On the evidence so far, then, the swarm is mostly the concert crowd. Agents move together because they hear the same band: the timetable, the goal, the room. The wave exists, but it is faint, and it travels only through messages that are read, mostly messages with a name on them.

For interpretability, this says where to look: find the field first, and read the context window for memory. For control, field levers, such as the kickoff text and context resets, are large. Message levers, such as \emph{nudges} (automatic prompts to an idle agent), are small, but a name multiplies the effect of a message about 19 times. \subsection*{What we cannot say yet}

All of these results are exploratory. Tests on the reserved data have confirmed almost none of them: of three early hypotheses tested there, one failed, one came out reversed, and one was inconclusive. The village is one swarm with one operator, so we cannot say which results hold elsewhere. Regime~I does not fit the picture: its talk swings 1.34 times (CI 1.18--1.52) more than the echo loop permits, and its timing partly follows the wall clock. We cannot yet explain either fact.
