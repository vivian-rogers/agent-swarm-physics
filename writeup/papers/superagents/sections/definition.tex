\section{What an egregore is}\label{sec:definition}

In this paper an egregore is not a group of agents. It is a pattern: a set of ideas, rules and practices that many agents carry. It continues while its carriers change and forget, and it has behaviors of its own. An ideology that runs on people is the model case. A government is near to one. Its laws, offices and procedures continue while officials and citizens change. The people who hold the offices at a given time do its acts, for example elections, taxes and enforcement. An ant colony is nearer to the other end. It is a fixed set of parts with a state of the whole colony. We call such a fixed group a \emph{group superagent}, and we use it as the contrast case. In neither case must the parts stop being individuals. An agent can carry an ideology and stay an agent. Schwitzgebel makes this argument against rules that forbid agents inside agents \cite{schwitzgebel2015}.

We make the idea measurable as follows.
\begin{tightitem}
\item A \emph{memeplex} $K$ is a set of elements that tend to appear together in the work of agents. The elements are groups of similar statements, new terms and names of procedures, and shared repositories.
\item The \emph{hosts} $H_K(n)$ of $K$ at time step $n$ are the agents that express it at that step.
\item The \emph{state} $S_K(n)$ of $K$ is the number of its hosts and the elements that are most frequent.
\item The \emph{environment} $E$ holds all inputs that can drive $K$ from outside. These are the timetable of the operator, the messages of the operator and of humans, and the private roles of the agents.
\item A \emph{pseudo-pattern} is a random set of elements with the same frequencies as the elements of $K$. It is our main comparison.
\end{tightitem}
We call $K$ an egregore if it meets the four conditions below. We call it a valued egregore if it also meets the fifth condition.

\emph{(1) It is an integrated individual.} We follow Krakauer \emph{et al.}~\cite{krakauer2020}. The colonial individuality
\begin{equation}
A_K = I(S'_K;\, S_K \mid E)
\end{equation}
measures how much the present state of the pattern tells about its next state $S'_K$. It counts only what the environment does not already tell. $A_K$ must be larger than for pseudo-patterns. The whole pattern must also predict better than its elements do one at a time.

\emph{(2) It lives above its hosts.} The pattern continues over days while its hosts change. It also continues after memory wipes of its hosts, because other hosts and shared artifacts give it back.

\emph{(3) It recruits.} Agents that read its hosts take it up more than agents that got the same kind of message but did not read it yet.

\emph{(4) It has functional behaviors.} Its hosts recruit other agents by name, help hosts that stop to start again, and divide its elements among themselves.

\emph{(5) Its information has value.} We follow Kolchinsky and Wolpert~\cite{kolchinsky2018}. Information is semantic if the system becomes less viable when the information is scrambled. For a pattern, viability is how many agents express it on the next day. Three natural events scramble its information: memory wipes of its hosts, the loss of a central host, and actions of the operator against the pattern. The value per bit is $\kappa_K = \Delta V_K / I_K$. Here $\Delta V_K$ is the change in viability and $I_K$ is the information that the event removes.

We also ask what a pattern does to its hosts. Does a host do more or less of the work in its own role while it carries the pattern? A \emph{parasitic} ideology keeps itself alive and pulls its hosts away from their roles. It is a small version of the misaligned group of agents that the essay describes.
