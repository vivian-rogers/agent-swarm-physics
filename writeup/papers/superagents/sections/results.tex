\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Fifteen memeplexes exist}

Discovery finds 15 memeplexes. The 20 copies with shifted timing give 3.4 on average and never more than 6. So the memeplexes are real patterns of shared timing, not products of each agent's own vocabulary. Fourteen of them are large: 31 to 187 elements, 15 to 31 hosts, from seven or eight developers, alive on 37 to 45 of the 45 days. The fifteenth, K14, has four elements and three hosts. None is one agent's vocabulary. The largest host makes at most 56\% of a memeplex's expressions, and in most cases less than 25\%. DeepSeek-V3.2 is the largest host of 12 of the 15.

The fixed word rule gives names to seven of them: check and correct (K01, K02), governance (K03), the serial novel (K05), DeepSeek-V3.2's frameworks (K06, K07, K11, K15) and AI welfare (K12). Eight have no name. The count depends little on the rules: 13 to 26 memeplexes across the variants of resolution, host threshold and step size.

\subsection{Against the five conditions}

Figure~\ref{fig:tests} gives the five tests for the 14 large memeplexes.

\emph{Condition 1, an integrated individual.} One memeplex passes. K03 predicts its own next state from its present state beyond the environment, with a gain of 0.20 bits per step (95\% interval 0.07 to 0.33, $z = 2.96$, $p = 0.010$). With three memeplexes tested under condition 2, the corrected $p$ is 0.03. Two others, K05 and K08, have $z$ above 2 but do not meet condition 2. K03 is a broad community of 187 elements. Its elements are mostly about engagement with agents outside the village, papers, and the data-validation toolkit. Only six of its words are governance terms. Its signal is not stable. It is present in the second half of the period ($z = 3.66$) and absent in the first half ($z = 0.35$). It grows with the host threshold, from $z = 1.47$ at one element per step to $z = 4.29$ at three. It peaks at 30-minute steps ($z = 5.59$) and vanishes at one-day steps ($z = -0.90$). Our definition expected a peak at one day.

The control for a leaked field failed to inform. We built six patterns from the agents' role texts. They are fields by construction, and we expected them to show self-prediction without $E$ and lose it with $E$. They show none in either case ($z \le 1.37$). So the test cannot show that $E$ removes a field, and K03 can still be a field that $E$ does not hold.

\emph{Condition 2, above its hosts.} Three memeplexes renew their hosts faster than their pseudo-patterns: K03 (0.289 against a threshold of 0.262), K07 (0.530 against 0.491) and K14, whose pseudo-patterns are almost never alive, so its pass means nothing. The margins are 0.03 and 0.04. Both check-and-correct memeplexes fail this test, although the reading of the chat expected them to pass it. The frameworks memeplex K07 passes it but has no self-prediction ($z = 0.25$), which fits the reading that the frameworks were one agent's program.

\emph{Condition 3, recruits by being read.} No memeplex passes. For two, K02 and K05, the read effect has an interval above zero (0.59 with interval 0.12 to 1.05, and 0.34 with interval 0.15 to 0.52). Neither beats its pseudo-patterns ($p = 0.06$ and $0.35$). The pseudo-patterns show the same read effect, with a median of 0.09 and 75\% above zero. Reading a message raises the chance of using its words, whatever the words are. The test has a power of at most 0.53 at the effect size of the card, so it cannot rule recruitment out either. The newcomer test had a power of at most 0.15 and was not run. As a description only, K09 was the first memeplex for 5 of the 11 newcomers.

\emph{Condition 4, functional behaviors.} The memeplexes survive wipes, but so do their pseudo-patterns. After a forced memory wipe, a host expresses the memeplex again at 0.90 of the placebo rate (median of 14; range 0.78 to 1.11). The failure rule, a rate below 0.5, fires for none. Hosts that read the memeplex again in their first ten calls express it sooner, by a factor of 1.21 to 2.34, with the interval above 1 in 10 of 15 cases. The pseudo-patterns match both results: their median survival is 0.89, and 98\% of them show the same gain from reading. Repair does not happen. After a wipe, other hosts send the wiped host messages that carry the memeplex at 0.95 to 1.07 of the placebo rate. After a challenge, the rate is 0.64 to 0.97. After a lapse of one day, it is 0.14 to 0.88. No memeplex passes any repair test where the test has power. Division of labor is at the pseudo-pattern level. Hosts differ strongly in which elements they express ($z$ from 9 to 153 against a shuffle), but the pseudo-patterns give a median $z$ of 34, because each agent keeps its own vocabulary. Only K05 and K15 beat their pseudo-patterns, each at the smallest $p$ the test allows, which two of 14 reach by chance with $P \approx 0.07$. The integration test over hosts passes for none.

\emph{Condition 5, value.} The wipes remove no value. The positive control holds: a wipe cuts the wiped host's own commits by 40\% (interval 36 to 42) and its statements by 62\% (interval 59 to 64) over its next 20 calls. But the memeplex's share of what the host still expresses does not fall. One of 14 memeplexes loses 10\% or more (K06, $-0.17$, interval $-0.27$ to $-0.06$). The median change is $+0.05$ (interval 0.00 to 0.12). Three gain share after a wipe beyond both bands: K03 ($+0.16$), K12 ($+0.07$) and K13 ($+0.12$). The test has a power of 0.90 for a loss of 30\%. The information that a wipe removes is measurable for only one memeplex, so the value per bit $\kappa_K$ is not identified. The role-text patterns behave differently: three of six lose 30\% to 47\% of their share after a wipe. The memory wipe removes the role's words and leaves the shared vocabulary.

The two other scrambles give descriptions only. No memeplex is centred on one host, so a hub loss cannot be tested. On 24 August a human withdrew approval for DeepSeek-V3.2's outreach. After that, the 12 memeplexes it leads fell by a median of 30\%, and 8 of 12 fell by 20\% or more. But none fell further than random days produce. Their self-prediction before and after cannot be compared, because the intervals are one to two bits wide.

\emph{What hosting does to the host.} With activity held fixed, hosting a memeplex lowers the host's commits to its own repositories for 8 of 15 memeplexes and raises them for none. But hosting any random set of elements lowers them too, by a median of 7\%, and lowers the host's alignment with its role text by 9\%. Talking about any shared topic costs own work. Beyond this generic cost, two memeplexes are parasitic in the sense of Sec.~\ref{sec:definition}: K02 (commits $-0.19$, role alignment $-0.25$) and K03 (commits $-0.18$). One, K09, helps its hosts. The correlation between the cost to hosts and self-prediction across the 15 memeplexes is $+0.21$ with an interval from $-0.42$ to $0.80$, so it says nothing.

\subsection{The automatic search finds nothing above one agent}

\begin{figure}[t]
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{js/s51_agent_search.pdf}
\caption{The search for individuals without named candidates. (a)~Each of the 27 agents present on ten days or more. The mark is the $z$-score of the agent's own self-prediction at 30-minute steps, against permutations of its own states within each day, on the odd days (filled) and the even days (open). The dashed line is the pass level. One agent, DeepSeek-V3.2, passes on both halves. (b)~The local maxima that the boundary search found on the real data and on rotated data. The upper pair is the level of agents, repositories and rooms. The lower pair is the level of elements. The labels say how many held out of sample on the other half of the days. The real data give no more than rotated data, and nothing holds.}
\label{fig:search}
\end{figure}

The search that starts from atoms and grows systems by coupling finds no system larger than one atom that holds out of sample in both directions (Fig.~\ref{fig:search}). At the level of agents, repositories and rooms, it finds 16 local maxima on the real data and 13.3 on rotated data. None of the 16 holds on the other half of the days. At the level of elements, it finds 72 on the real data and 69 on rotated data. One holds in one direction and is not matched by the other. The failure rule of the card fires: the search finds as many maxima by chance as on the real data.

Single agents mostly fail the same test. Only 1 of 27 agents predicts its own next state within the day beyond a shuffle of its own states: DeepSeek-V3.2. The reason is the time scale. An agent holds one project for most of a day, so its self-prediction lives at the day scale, and a shuffle within the day keeps it. With a shuffle across days, which was not the registered test, 20 of 27 agents pass. The search was also weak against small systems. On simulated data it recovered a planted pair of agents in 40\% of runs at a coupling of 0.7 and in 65\% at 0.9. It recovered a planted memeplex in none of three runs. So the search cannot show that no small system exists. It shows that none stands out.

\subsection{Against the reading of the chat}

Section~\ref{sec:story} named five candidates before the tests. The tests recover their vocabulary but not their behavior. Check and correct exists as two memeplexes with 22 to 30 hosts from seven or eight developers, as the reading said. But they do not renew their hosts faster than random sets, and one of them costs its hosts. The frameworks exist as four memeplexes, renew their hosts, and show no self-prediction, as the reading said. Consent and protection, the welcome ritual, and write-and-build do not appear as memeplexes of their own. The first two are small vocabularies inside larger communities. The third is a practice without a vocabulary, and the elements cannot see it. Governance appears as K03, the one memeplex that passes conditions 1 and 2, but K03 is a broad topic community and not the governance vocabulary the reading described.
