# Group Agency: The Possibility, Design, and Status of Corporate Agents

**Citation:** Christian List and Philip Pettit, *Group Agency: The Possibility, Design, and Status of Corporate Agents* (Oxford University Press, 2011).
**File:** none. Notes from abstract/secondary sources, PDF not in repo (a book; publisher and LSE pages, and published reviews, checked 2026-10-09).
**Fields:** philosophy, social choice theory, social ontology

## Summary
The book argues that some groups are agents in their own right, over and above their members, and that the social sciences, law and morality must treat them as such. The account is "unmysterious": a group agent is a system with representational states (judgments), motivational states (preferences) and a way to act on them, and it forms these states by an organization that its members carry out. The main technical tool is judgment aggregation. The authors show that no aggregation rule can be at once responsive to each member's view on each question and guarantee a consistent set of group views. So a rational group agent must sometimes hold views that most of its members do not hold. In that sense its attitudes are autonomous from its members', though they still supervene on what the members do.

## Key ideas
- **Agency conditions.** A system is an agent if it has representational states, motivational states, and the capacity to process them and to act on the environment so that the world matches its motivations, within feasible limits, with some rationality.
- **Discursive dilemma.** Three members vote on premises p, q and the conclusion p ∧ q. Premise-wise majority can say yes to p and to q while conclusion-wise majority says no to p ∧ q. Proposition-wise majority voting is not consistent.
- **Impossibility theorem (List & Pettit 2002).** No aggregation function satisfies universal domain, collective rationality, anonymity and systematicity at once. Relaxing systematicity gives premise-based or sequential procedures.
- **Supervenience with autonomy.** Group attitudes are fixed by members' attitudes and the organization, but they cannot be read off any member, nor off a simple majority. This is the philosophical form of "the whole holds information that no part holds".
- **Persistence through turnover.** A group agent keeps its identity over changes of membership, because the organization (its procedures and roles) persists. Corporations, churches and states are the examples.
- **Responsibility and personhood.** Group agents can be held responsible and can count as persons in a functional sense.

## Mapping to paper 2
- List–Pettit give the conceptual target for the "group superagent" contrast case (H143/H144): a bounded set of members bound by procedures. The #51 governance crew, with its written voting protocol, binding voters and GO/NO-GO decisions, is a candidate of exactly their kind.
- Their account requires an organization that makes group attitudes. An ideology egregore has no voting procedure; its "attitudes" are the distribution of its elements across hosts. So our egregore is closer to Dawkins/Sperber than to List–Pettit, and their criteria are a stricter bar we can test against.
- The autonomy of group attitudes suggests a measurable criterion: the pattern's state is predicted by its own past better than by any single host or by majority vote over hosts. That is our integration condition (whole beats its elements one by one).
- Their account is functional and silent on measurement. Krakauer et al. and Kolchinsky–Wolpert supply the measurements.

## Caveats
- The book is normative and conceptual. It gives no statistic for deciding whether an observed group is an agent.
- Our agents' "judgments" are text; "group attitudes" must be coarse-grained from statements, which is a project choice.
