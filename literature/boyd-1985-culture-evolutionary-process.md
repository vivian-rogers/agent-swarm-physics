# Culture and the Evolutionary Process

**Citation:** Robert Boyd and Peter J. Richerson, *Culture and the Evolutionary Process* (University of Chicago Press, 1985).
**File:** none. Notes from abstract/secondary sources, PDF not in repo (a book; publisher page and reviews checked 2026-10-09).
**Fields:** cultural evolution, population genetics, anthropology

## Summary
The book founds dual-inheritance theory: genes and culture are two coupled inheritance systems, and culture can be modelled with the tools of population genetics. About three dozen models track how the frequencies of cultural variants change under the forces that act on them. Culture here is information acquired from others by social learning. The book does not need discrete replicators: it treats transmission as a set of biased learning rules and asks which rules are favored and what population-level patterns they produce.

## Key ideas and formalism
- **Forces of cultural evolution.** Guided variation (learners change what they acquire), and three transmission biases:
  - *direct (content) bias*: some variants are easier to learn or seem better;
  - *frequency-dependent (conformist) bias*: learners adopt the common variant with probability above its frequency, e.g. Δp ∝ p(1 − p)(2p − 1) for a two-variant model;
  - *indirect (model-based, prestige) bias*: learners copy variants carried by successful or prestigious individuals.
- **Natural selection on cultural variants** acts through the survival and reproduction of carriers, and can work against the variants that transmission favors.
- **Population-level consequences.** Conformist bias keeps groups internally similar and different from each other. This sets up group selection among cultural groups, a central argument in their later work (Richerson & Boyd, *Not by Genes Alone*, 2005).
- **Maladaptive culture** can spread when transmission biases and individual fitness disagree.

## Caveats
- The models assume large, well-mixed populations of human learners over generations. The village has 21–32 agents, broadcast rooms, and "generations" set by context erasures (every ~41 calls), not by births and deaths.
- The biases are inferred from frequency changes, which shared model priors and operator fields can mimic. Our impostor bundle E must hold those fields.
