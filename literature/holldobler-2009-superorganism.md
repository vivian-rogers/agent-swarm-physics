# The Superorganism: The Beauty, Elegance, and Strangeness of Insect Societies

**Citation:** Bert Hölldobler and Edward O. Wilson, *The Superorganism: The Beauty, Elegance, and Strangeness of Insect Societies* (W. W. Norton, New York, 2009), xxi + 522 pp.
**File:** none. Notes from abstract/secondary sources, PDF not in repo (a book; catalogue records and reviews checked 2026-10-09).
**Fields:** entomology, sociobiology, collective behavior

## Summary
The book treats a colony of ants, bees, wasps or termites as a superorganism: a society whose members are bound by division of labor, communication and self-organized collective decisions, so that the colony behaves as one unit of function and of selection. It reviews the genetic and physiological basis of castes, the chemical and tactile communication systems, the algorithms by which colonies forage, build nests and allocate labor without central control, and the evolution of eusociality. It revives the colony-as-organism idea of W. M. Wheeler ("The ant-colony as an organism", *J. Morphol.* 22, 307–325, 1911) with modern data, and argues that colony-level selection drives social evolution.

## Key ideas
- **Superorganism criteria (by analogy with an organism):** reproductive division of labor (queens and workers), non-reproductive division of labor among workers (castes, age polyethism), integrated communication, colony-level homeostasis (nest temperature, food stores), and a colony life cycle.
- **Sociogenesis.** Colony-level traits arise from individual rules: response thresholds for tasks, pheromone trails, quorum sensing in nest choice. No individual holds the colony's plan.
- **Unit of selection.** The colony's success, not the worker's, is what selection sees. The authors argue for multilevel selection.
- **Bounded membership.** A colony is a closed, mostly related set of individuals with a colony odor; non-members are rejected.
