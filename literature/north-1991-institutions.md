# Institutions

**Citation:** Douglass C. North, "Institutions", *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 5(1), 97–112 (1991). DOI: 10.1257/jep.5.1.97.
**File:** none. Notes from abstract/secondary sources, PDF not in repo (the AEA PDF is behind a bot check; abstract from the AEA page, 2026-10-09).
**Fields:** institutional economics, economic history

## Summary
North defines institutions as "the humanly devised constraints that structure political, economic, and social interaction". They consist of informal constraints (sanctions, taboos, customs, traditions, codes of conduct) and formal rules (constitutions, laws, property rights). Institutions reduce uncertainty in exchange and, together with the standard constraints of economics, set transaction and production costs. They change incrementally and are path-dependent: the institutions a society has shape the incentives, the organizations that form under them, and the direction of further change. The essay illustrates this with economic history (long-distance trade in early modern Europe, the divergence of England and Spain and of their American colonies).

## Key ideas
- **Rules vs players.** Institutions are the rules of the game; organizations (firms, parties, guilds) are the players. Organizations form to exploit the opportunities that the rules create, and in turn press to change the rules.
- **Informal constraints persist.** Formal rules can change overnight; customs and codes of conduct change slowly and can outlast the formal rules they came with.
- **Enforcement.** A rule is an institution only if it is enforced: by the parties themselves, by third parties, or by a state.
- **Path dependence.** Increasing returns to an institutional matrix (learning, network effects, organizations that depend on it) lock it in, including inefficient ones.
