\hwhat{Do \#51's private, partly conflicting roles make the swarm a spin glass? Tested on sub-periods 51a--e (45 non-holdout days) against shared-objective weeks \#38, \#40, \#44, after synthetic validation (the balance index separates factions from random signs; SK and a heterogeneous ferromagnet cannot be told apart under common drive). Content couplings are real in 51b and 51c ($\rho_{\rm split}=0.46$, $p=0.003$) but 85--94\% positive ($\hat\kappa=4$--10); the balance index is indeterminate. Same-role rivals co-move \emph{more} than unrelated pairs ($T_{\rm SR}=+0.048$, $p=0.02$), the homophily rival. Positions persist ($M=0.5$--0.75) without collective switching ($W\approx1$). Contrast weeks are no less ferromagnetic; talk couplings sit at the noise floor. Holdout test (\#51 tail) written, \textbf{not run}.}

\htelos{Practically, little. ``Spin glass'' was the exciting reading of mixed-motive swarms: frustrated, history-dependent, hard to steer. The data say something duller and more usable. Assigned roles act as fixed fields that pin each agent's topic, the shared room adds a weak positive pull, and same-objective agents drift together. For an operator, roles, not inter-agent conflict, set what such a swarm talks about, and there are no hidden collective states to escape. Caveat: embeddings measure topic, not stance, so strategic opposition could be invisible here. The diagnostics port to other logs; the spin-glass vocabulary is not earned.}

\hbottom{\#51 is not a spin glass: private roles make it a random-field system with weak, mostly positive coupling, where rivals align rather than repel and the swarm never switches collectively.}
