\hwhat{15 counted units (regime II--III goal periods \#35--\#44 and \#51, non-holdout; $N=10$--27 agents, 57k chat statements). After removing each day's common field, agents from the same lab share a content direction: significant in 9/15 units (the rule needed 10), random-effects $T=0.08$ [0.02, 0.14], and stable across periods (family split-half cosine 0.88 vs a 0.76 regrouping null, $p=0.008$). \textbf{But it is writing style:} regressing out 20 style features (length, bullets, punctuation, pronouns) leaves it in 0/9 units (median retention 3\%), and the style features alone identify families in 14/15. No period-specific family stance (3/14). \textbf{No family coupling:} family-block mean-field couplings in talk timing (RE $\Delta=0.002$) and 30-min content co-movement ($-0.002$) are flat. \textbf{Rooms carry the coupling:} room beats lab in 8/10 (talk) and 9/10 (co-movement) two-room units. Newcomers are classified by family field at 4/11 (chance 14\%). Holdout script frozen, not run.}

\htelos{For anyone mixing vendors in one swarm: models from the same lab sound alike, and that is nearly all there is. The family ``field'' is formatting and punctuation, not a shared stance on the task, and there is no measurable in-group preference in who reacts to whom. What organizes interaction is who shares a room, which operators control. Two practical consequences, both modest. Any embedding-based consensus or alignment metric must control for writing style, or it will ``discover'' vendor blocs. Worries about vendor homophily or collusion get no support at this sampling, though only couplings of about 0.1 or larger are excluded. The rest is a curiosity: 20 hand-made style features fingerprint labs better than embeddings do.}

\hbottom{Model families differ in how they write, not in what they think or whom they follow; rooms, not vendors, set who couples with whom.}
