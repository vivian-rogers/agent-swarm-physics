\hwhat{H35 treats the auto-nudger as a feedback controller, i.e.\ a Maxwell demon. It measures the agent-state information in nudges, the activity they buy, and their distance from the best use of those bits. The test covers 13 non-holdout periods and two natural experiments (dated predictions; synthetic recovery checked first). G51 is the only well-powered period. There, a first nudge buys 1.45 [0.72, 2.20] extra active minutes and uses 1.4 bits of state. That is 38\% of the work those bits could buy ($\eta_{SU}=0.38$ [$-0.08$, 0.68]); only 15\% of the bits are needed. Among pausing agents, the nudger does worse than random. It sends 38\% of nudges to traps $\ge 10$ pauses deep, which return 0.5 min each (2.2--2.6 min at $k=1$--9). Nudges add 0.5\% of active minutes. Switch-on (NE10) and a silent stop on 08-20 do not show them. Under the old 12-h pause default, nudges wake agents at any trap age. \textbf{Round 1b} (work ledger, ledger calls): the nudge buys glances, not committed work or sustained runs. The holdout is not run.}

\hmodel{Models 04 (Kolchinsky--Wolpert) and 02. The system $X$ is an agent's state: idle time, pause timing and trap age $k$ (pause-chain length). The demon is the nudge $M\in\{0,1\}$ at rate $r$. The work $g(x)$ is the extra active minutes in 30 min that a nudge causes in state $x$. The value of information $\Delta V$ is the work beyond random nudging. The frontier $V^*(R)$ is the most work that any policy with $R$ bits can buy. $s$ is the half-range of $g$ and plays the role of $k_BT$. $p_k$ is the probability of acting at the pause expiry.}

\hmath{\vspace{-5pt}\begin{gather*}
\Delta V(\pi)=\mathbb{E}[\pi g]-r\,\mathbb{E}[g],\qquad I=I(X;M)\\
V^*(R)=\max_{I\le R}\Delta V\ \Rightarrow\ \pi_\beta=\sigma\big(\mathrm{logit}\,r+\beta(g-\lambda)\big)\\
\eta_{SU}=\Delta V_{\log}/V^*(I_{\log}),\quad \eta_{KW}=R^*(\Delta V_{\log})/I_{\log}\\
\Delta V\le s\sqrt{2rI}\ \ (\text{Donsker--Varadhan})\\
\mathrm{logit}\,p_k=\alpha_i+\theta\ln k+(\gamma+\lambda\ln k)\,\mathbb{1}[\text{nudge}]
\end{gather*}\vspace{-7pt}}

\htelos{An operator who runs an idle-nudger can act on this. Nudge each pause chain once, at the first or second re-pause, and only while the agent is paused. Stop re-nudging agents stuck in long chains: they barely respond and need a reset or a new task. In G51 this policy gives $\times$2.3 active minutes per nudge at the same budget. It does not buy committed work. The nudger buys only about 0.5\% of agent activity, so even a perfect policy moves the swarm little. The thermodynamic frame works as accounting (frontier, ceiling, efficiency), not as physics. The policy result rests on one short-pause period.}

\hbottom{The nudger is an inefficient Maxwell demon for activity (once-early nudging buys 2--3$\times$ more active minutes) and a worthless one for work (+0.22 commits/h, n.s.).}
