\hwhat{H40 asks whether agents respond to each other per model call or per minute. A reply-hazard model runs on each recipient's real call schedule (context ledger, DQ2 reply labels) in 33 non-holdout periods. Simulated replies first show that the model tells the two clocks apart. In the computer-use scaffold (regimes II--III, 11 periods), coupling runs on the call clock. A call that spans more wall time carries no more reply hazard: $\eta=0.00\pm0.05$ in G51, and $-0.03$ at timer wakes fixed in advance. In G51 the per-call coupling is flat in cadence ($s=+0.11$ [$-0.03$, 0.25]) and set by model family. The per-hour coupling scales with call rate (slope 1.0), so slow agents are heavy spins. In regime I (22 periods) the call clock fails: $\eta=+0.51$ [0.41, 0.61]. Natives: G51 yes, NE41 mixed, G18 and NE14 no. Round 2 explains the negative $\eta$ of G38 and G44 ($-0.57$, $-0.34$). Long tool calls are followed by fewer talk calls ($\eta_{\rm talk}=-0.28$). Given a talk call, the span elasticity is $-0.11$ [$-0.16$, $-0.06$] in all four regime-III periods tested. The holdout is not run.}

\hmodel{Models 02 (kinetic Ising, Glauber attempt rates) and 09. The degree of freedom is the reply of recipient $i$. Each model call $c$ of $i$ is an update attempt. After $i$ reads message $m$ at call 1, it replies at call $n$ with hazard $h_n$. $\alpha_i$ is the per-call coupling. $e_n$ is the wall time that call $n$ spans; $a_n$ is the age of $m$; $b_n$ counts newer messages (burial); $k_n$ is the batch size. The order parameter $\eta$ is the exposure-time elasticity: 0 on the call clock, 1 on the wall clock. $r_i$ is the call rate, and $\varepsilon(T)$ the cadence elasticity of a reply within $T$.}

\hmath{\vspace{-5pt}\begin{gather*}
\mathrm{cloglog}\,h_n=\alpha_i+\phi\ln n+\psi\ln a_n+\chi\ln(1{+}b_n)+\eta\ln e_n+\delta\ln(1{+}k_n)+\dots\\
\text{wall clock: } h_n=1-e^{-\lambda e_n}\ \Rightarrow\ \eta=1;\qquad \text{call clock: }\eta=0\\
J^{\mathrm{hour}}_i=J^{\mathrm{call}}_i\,r_i^{\,1-\eta},\qquad \alpha_i=c+s\ln r_i\ \ (s=0:\ \text{per-call invariant})\\
\varepsilon(T)=\partial\ln P(\text{reply}\le T)/\partial\ln(\text{speed-up})
\end{gather*}\vspace{-7pt}}

\htelos{In a computer-use agent loop, an agent's wall-time coupling to others is its per-call coupling (a model-family trait) times its call rate. Long tool calls, long pauses and slow models make agents ``heavy''. In G51 the slowest fifth answer within 5 min 8$\times$ less often than the fastest fifth. Cadence is a coupling knob that needs no prompt change. To couple an agent faster, halve its call interval: 5-min coupling rises by about 50\% (G51 32\%), less at 30 min. Use short pause timers for coordinators. To decouple an agent, give it long batch jobs. The rule does not transfer to a scheduler-driven chat loop (regime I), where time itself carries part of the coupling. The evidence is mostly observational; the clean cadence intervention (NE20) is in the holdout.}

\hbottom{In the computer-use scaffold, agents couple per model call, not per minute: per-hour coupling is a family trait times call rate, and regime I breaks this.}

\hcaveats{Regime I is unexplained. Its positive $\eta$ survives logged start times, short spans and the placement test. The generation-vs-wait split has only 274 logged replies, too few to decide. The talk split conditions on talk, which a read message can cause (H50). The residual $-0.11$ given talk has no identified cause. Agents partly choose their cadence; only the pause-timer dose is quasi-exogenous. The cross-sectional G51 slope (1.0) exceeds the model's speed-up elasticity (0.40), so composition adds to cadence. Reply labels are DQ2 parents; rank-2/3 candidates are mostly unlabelled, so multi-target replies are undercounted, most for slow agents with big pools. The NE41 ratio fails for recency reasons found post hoc. The card has two post hoc checks; the holdout is not run.}
